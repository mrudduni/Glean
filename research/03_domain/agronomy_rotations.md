# Agronomy the tool must encode

This file lists the crop science rules and numbers our rotation tool needs. Each number comes from an Extension publication, FAO, NRCS, or a peer-reviewed paper, listed under Sources. Where we could not confirm a figure on the source page, the text says "not confirmed." Most numbers come from the U.S. Corn Belt and Great Plains, so the tool must flag them for local review elsewhere.

## Nitrogen from legumes

Legumes host rhizobia bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air. The following crop gets some of that N, but the size of the credit depends on the legume, the stand, the soil, and the weather.

| Preceding crop | N credit to next corn crop | Source and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soybean | 45 lb N/acre on non-sandy soils, 25 lb N/acre on sandy soils | University of Nebraska. If soybean yield was under 30 bu/acre, credit 1 lb N per bushel. |
| Soybean (research mean) | About 55 lb N/acre dryland, 65 lb N/acre irrigated | 20 years of USDA-ARS work in Nebraska, cited by UNL. UNL still recommends 45 to limit risk. |
| Alfalfa | 90 to 150 lb N/acre on fine soils, 40 to 100 lb N/acre on sandy soils | UNL. Use the high end for stands over 4 plants per square foot and the low end under 1.5 plants per square foot. Some states also give a second-year credit. |
| Hairy vetch (cover crop) | Often replaces up to 100 lb N/acre; 40 to 180 lb N/acre per year in one 15-year Illinois case | SARE, Managing Cover Crops Profitably. Vetch adds about 2 to 3 lb N/acre per sunny day after spring growth starts, peaking near early bloom. |
| Hairy vetch plus cereal rye mix | 50 to 100 lb N/acre credit before corn | SARE. |
| Crimson clover (cover crop) | 70 to 150 lb total N/acre in the plant (not a fertilizer replacement value) | OSU Extension, using MCCC data. |

UNL explains that much of the soybean credit comes from soybean residue breaking down faster than corn residue and tying up less soil N, not from extra fixed N. A soybean crop removes more N in grain than it fixes in many cases. The tool should present soybean "credits" as a reduced fertilizer need, not as N added to the soil.

## Cover crops and their roles

| Cover crop | Type | Main roles | Key numbers | Cautions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cereal rye | Winter-hardy grass | Scavenges leftover N, protects soil over winter, suppresses weeds | Takes up 25 to 50 lb N/acre typically, up to 100 lb N/acre (SARE). An Iowa study held N leaching to under 5 lb N/acre from September to May with rye or rye and oats overseeded into soybean. | Can tie up N for a following corn crop; plant no deeper than 2 inches. |
| Hairy vetch | Winter annual legume | Fixes N, weed-suppressing mulch | See N table above. | Dry soil lowers germination; needs late termination for full N. |
| Crimson clover | Winter annual legume | N source, erosion control, pollinator habitat | 3,500 to 5,500 lb dry matter/acre/yr; 70 to 150 lb N/acre (OSU). Poor host for soybean cyst nematode. | May winter-kill; does not tolerate flooding. |
| Radish (forage or oilseed) | Brassica, winter-killed | Fall N scavenging, deep taproot, weed suppression | Wisconsin trials: 17 to 180 lb N/acre uptake, average 76; roots often reach 3 ft. | Wisconsin found no clear corn yield benefit and little N passed to corn; not advised alone on highly erodible land or sandy soils. Best mixed with rye. |

Remote-sensing studies give a reality check on yield effects. Seifert et al. (2018) found average yield gains of only 0.65 percent for maize and 0.35 percent for soybean on cover-cropped Midwest fields. Deines et al. (2023) estimated average yield losses of 5.5 percent for maize and 3.5 percent for soybean on fields with three or more years of cover crops. The tool should present cover crops mainly as soil, water quality, and erosion tools with a possible small yield cost, not as a yield booster.

## Deep-rooting crops

Deep roots pull water and nutrients from lower soil layers and leave channels that help water infiltrate. The Wisconsin radish publication reports radish roots commonly reaching 3 ft, and Maryland research showed radish taproots can penetrate compacted layers. A Texas study (Bhattarai et al., 2020) found sorghum and pearl millet drew water from 0 to 1 m depth versus 0 to 0.6 m for corn. Alfalfa is widely described as deep rooted, but we did not confirm a specific rooting depth on a source page (not confirmed).

## Drought-tolerant crops

| Crop | Evidence | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grain sorghum | Rainfed trial in central Nebraska (2009 to 2011): sorghum produced at least 5.5 bu grain per inch of water, corn at least 4.3, soybean at least 2.0. On a three-year average sorghum gave 1.2 bu/inch more than corn and 3.5 bu/inch more than soybean. In 2011 sorghum used 17.3 inches of ET versus 22.0 for corn. | UNL CropWatch |
| Grain sorghum | Seasonal water need 17 to 25 inches in the Nebraska Panhandle, versus 23 to 26 for corn. | UNL CropWatch 2026 |
| Proso millet | NRCS describes proso as heat and drought tolerant and suited to dry upland soils. | NRCS Plant Materials |
| Pearl millet | Utah State Extension calls it one of the most drought-tolerant grains. | USU Extension |

Sorghum and millet suit a "drought adaptation" rotation option, where the tool swaps some corn years for a crop with a lower water need. The tool must flag market access, harvest equipment, and herbicide options as local review items.

## Pest and disease cycle breaks

### Corn rootworm

Northern and western corn rootworm lay eggs in the soil in late summer; larvae hatch the next spring and feed on corn roots for 4 to 6 weeks. Rotating to a non-host crop such as soybean has long broken this cycle. Both species have adapted in some regions, according to Iowa State:

- Northern corn rootworm shows extended diapause, where some eggs stay dormant for two or more winters and hatch when corn returns in a corn-soybean rotation. This variant occurs in Iowa, Minnesota, Nebraska, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.
- Western corn rootworm has a soybean variant: females lay eggs in soybean fields, and the larvae hatch into next year's corn. This variant occurs in Illinois, Iowa, Indiana, Michigan, and Ohio.

The tool should treat a two-year corn-soybean rotation as a partial break in these states and point to longer rotations (for example, adding a small grain) as a stronger break.

### Soybean cyst nematode (SCN)

SCN is a major soybean pest. Iowa State recommends SCN-resistant varieties in rotation with corn, a non-host. Almost all Iowa varieties use PI 88788 resistance, which has lost much of its effect after about 25 years of use; ISU found yields of PI 88788 varieties can trail Peking-resistant varieties by more than 22 bu/acre. The tool should credit any non-host year as a break and flag variety choice and soil sampling as local review items.

### Continuous cropping penalties

Seifert et al. (2017) analyzed 748,374 commercial field yields from six Corn Belt states (2007 to 2012). Continuous corn averaged a 4.3 percent yield penalty (1.7 percent under irrigation), and continuous soybean averaged 10.3 percent. The soybean penalty grew with each extra year of soybean, up to about 15 percent after five years, which points to disease buildup.

## Corn-soybean rotation effect

| Study | Corn benefit | Soybean benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wisconsin and Minnesota long-term trials, 29 environments (Lauer, Porter, Oplinger, 1997) | Rotated corn yielded 13 percent more than continuous corn; first-year corn 15 percent more | Rotated soybean 10 percent more than continuous; first-year soybean 18 percent more |
| Ohio State, 29 years by location | 13 percent | 11 percent |
| Erickson (2008) literature summary, cited by UNL | 7.8 percent average | 8 to 17 percent across studies |
| Seifert et al. (2017), commercial fields | 4.3 percent continuous corn penalty | 10.3 percent continuous soybean penalty |

The Wisconsin study found the rotation effect is larger in poor years. In low-yield environments rotated crops often beat continuous crops by more than 25 percent, while in high-yield environments the gain was usually under 15 percent. This matters for climate risk: rotation acts partly as insurance.

Diverse rotations add more. Bowles et al. (2020) pooled 11 long-term North American experiments and found that more diverse rotations raised maize yields by 28.1 percent on average and cut drought-year losses by 14 to 90 percent.

FAO-56 Table 11 gives stage lengths. For example, soybean in the central USA runs 20, 30 to 35, 60, and 25 days (initial, development, mid, late; about 140 days, May planting), and millet in the central USA runs 20, 30, 55, 35 days (140 days, April). The tool can build a daily Kc curve from these stage lengths and then shift stages using observed NDVI phenology.

## Erosion and the RUSLE factors

RUSLE2 estimates average annual sheet and rill erosion as A = R x K x LS x C x P, in tons per acre per year (Penn State Extension).

| Factor | Meaning | How the tool can estimate it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R | Rainfall amount and intensity (erosivity) | GPM IMERG rainfall with an erosivity relation, or published R maps |
| K | Soil erodibility, tied to texture and stoniness | SSURGO K factor |
| LS | Slope length and steepness | SRTM or 3DEP elevation |
| C | Cover and management: residue, tillage, rotation, cover crops | Crop sequence plus bare-soil months from NDVI; this is the factor rotations change |
| P | Support practices: contouring, strip cropping, buffers | Farmer input; default 1.0 |

NRCS compares A with the soil loss tolerance T, which ranges from 1 to 5 tons per acre per year depending on the soil (MSU Institute of Water Research). Rotation choices change only C (and sometimes P), so the tool can report relative erosion between strategies even when R, K, and LS are rough.

## Soil organic matter

The NRCS Soil Conditioning Index scores a system's organic matter trend from three parts: organic matter inputs (40 percent weight), field operations (40 percent), and erosion (20 percent). A negative SCI means organic matter is predicted to decline. Biomass returned to the soil, fewer tillage passes, and lower erosion all raise the score. Renwick et al. (2021) linked long-term rotation diversity to better maize drought resistance through higher soil organic matter. The popular claim that each 1 percent of organic matter stores a fixed extra volume of water per acre is not confirmed, and the tool should avoid it.

## NRCS soil health principles

USDA (farmers.gov) lists four principles: minimize disturbance, maximize soil cover, maximize biodiversity, and maximize the presence of living roots. Many NRCS and state materials add a fifth, integrate livestock (for example, Kansas and Nevada Extension). Each maps to a measurable rotation trait:

| Principle | Rotation metric the tool can compute |
| --- | --- |
| Minimize disturbance | Tillage passes per year (farmer input) |
| Maximize soil cover | Months with bare soil, from NDVI or bare-soil index |
| Maximize biodiversity | Number of crop species and families in the rotation; rotation complexity index |
| Maximize living roots | Months per year with a living crop or cover crop |
| Integrate livestock | Grazed cover crop or forage years (farmer input) |

## Growing degree days and frost dates

The standard corn growing degree day method (Purdue, citing Gilmore and Rogers 1958 and Barger 1969) is GDD = ((Tmax + Tmin) / 2) - 50, in degrees F. Daily highs above 86 F are set to 86, and daily lows below 50 F are set to 50. A typical central Indiana season from late April to late September collects about 2,800 GDD. In Celsius the limits are 10 C and 30 C.

NOAA NCEI freeze tables use three thresholds: 36 F for frost, 32 F for freeze, and 28 F for hard freeze (NC State Extension). They give the date of last spring and first fall events at 10 to 90 percent probability. NC State suggests the 10 percent date for risk-averse planning and the 90 percent date for aggressive planning. The current published normals use 1981 to 2010, with updates based on 1991 to 2020 expected. Our tool can compute its own frost probabilities from NASA POWER daily Tmin for any point.

## Heat stress

Corn development slows above 86 F (30 C), which is why GDD caps at that value (OSU and Purdue). For yield damage the common approach counts degree days above a threshold; Schlenker and Roberts (2009) found yields fall sharply above about 29 C for corn, 30 C for soybean, and 32 C for cotton. The tool should report heat degree days above these thresholds during flowering.
